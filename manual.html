OBD Dash

Extra gauges for a car that doesn't have them, on an Android phone.

Your car measures far more than its dashboard shows. A small adapter plugs into a socket under the steering wheel, your phone reads what the car reports over Bluetooth, and this app displays it — temperatures, voltage, fuel economy, and a plain-English explanation of any warning light.

Leave the phone in the car and it records every drive on its own. There is no account to make and nothing is collected about you.

What you need

An Android phone

Version 12 or newer.

An OBD2 adapter

Bluetooth, sold everywhere. The inexpensive ones mostly work fine.

A car built since ~2001

Almost all of them have the socket this uses.

Getting started
1

Plug in the adapter

The socket is under the dashboard on the driver's side, usually near your knees.

2

Pair it once

In your phone's own Bluetooth settings. If it asks for a PIN, it is nearly always 1234 or 0000.

3

Choose it in the app

Settings, then Adapter, and pick it from the paired list. Not listed? Pair it in your phone's Bluetooth settings first, then tap Refresh paired.

Small circled i marks, everywhere in the app

Tap one and the app explains what that reading is and what a healthy value looks like. They sit on every reading that isn't obvious, so nothing here needs prior knowledge of engines. The Terminal theme marks the same readings with a dotted underline instead.

The cockpit

Everything on one screen. Nothing needs a tap while moving.

Speed, revs, load, economy, coolant and battery on one panel, with the drive's totals underneath. It turns sideways with the phone, and one swipe brings up Diagnostics. Pick its look in Settings, under Theme.

The strip along the top

Always there: whether the phone has the car, whether the engine is running, and whether the check engine light is on. Terminal keeps it at the foot, as a prompt, and Recall under the trip figures.

Anything serious takes it over, pulls you back to the dashboard and chimes: twice for overheating or a charging voltage that is too high, once for a check engine light. Silent mode is respected. A battery that is not being charged shows in amber without a chime; the car's own battery light is the alarm for that.

Reading it at a glance

Every theme is dark, so the screen does not light up the cabin at night. Green means a reading is where it should be, amber worth a look, red stop.

The display stays awake while the engine runs. If the phone isn't plugged in, turn that off in Settings.

No adapter yet? Settings has a Simulated drive that runs every screen with no adapter and no car, and stays out of your real trip history.

After the drive

Every drive is saved, and a slow change shows up in the charts.

History

Reached from the clock icon in the dashboard footer. Every drive is listed with distance, economy and fuel cost.

The two charts are the reason this screen exists. Battery voltage drifting down over weeks, or peak coolant drifting up, is worth looking into. Neither shows up in one reading, only as a line.

The second keeps economy in traffic apart from economy on the open road. Economy moves with weather, traffic and how you drive, so read it as a trend rather than a verdict on the engine.

It survives a new phone

History exports to a spreadsheet and reads back in. Or turn on Back up to Google Drive: sign in once on each phone and every drive, car and cost stays in step.

One drive in detail

Tap any trip for what it cost, time moving versus stopped, the hottest the engine got, the highest speed and the lowest the battery dropped.

Alerts raised lists anything that went wrong, even briefly — a charging fault that came and went is something you would otherwise never know about. Worth a look is gentler: a reading outside its usual range, without being a fault.

More than one car

Cars are added and chosen in Settings, under Car. The name at the top of History narrows the list to one car, and its charts and costs follow.

A drive saved under the wrong car can be opened and sent across with Move to another car, or deleted from there. Deleting a car takes its trips with it.

Warnings, faults and inspections

A warning light tells you something is wrong, the app tells you what.

Fault codes, in plain English

When the check engine light comes on, the car has stored a code explaining why. Tap Read on Diagnostics and you get it as a sentence, not the five-character number a cheap code reader gives you.

It also saves a snapshot of what the engine was doing at the time. The same code at cold idle and at motorway speed often points somewhere different, and the snapshot is what a mechanic will want to see.

Before you clear a warning light

The app can clear a stored fault, behind a confirmation. Clearing fixes nothing — if the cause remains, the car records it again, often within a day.

It also erases that snapshot and resets the self-tests below, so the car will fail an inspection until it has been driven enough to finish them. Useful after a repair; a bad idea the morning of an inspection.

Diagnostics, with every self-test passed and no stored faults.

Check this before you drive to a testing station

Your car runs its own emissions self-tests as you drive, and an inspection fails if they haven't all finished — even with nothing wrong with the car. Disconnecting the battery or clearing a fault resets them.

Diagnostics lists them and says whether you are ready. If any are outstanding, a mix of town and open-road driving over a day or two usually finishes them.

If a reading is missing, or something isn't working

Settings reports what your car and adapter turned out to be capable of, including any readings your car doesn't provide — normal, and it varies by model. Share log files bundles the app's own records to be sent on; nothing identifying goes in them.

Fuel, costs and units

Tell the app what you pay per litre or gallon and every drive shows its cost. Metric or imperial changes only the display, never what is recorded. FUEL CUT in place of economy is normal: coasting, or a hybrid on its battery, burns nothing.

Setting it up, and living with it

Set up once and you can stop opening the app altogether.

It can start itself

Turn on Watch the car in the background and the app connects on its own, records the drive and lets go when you park. A notification shows what it is doing and can stop it.

Wake the screen when the engine starts is the more intrusive half: leave the phone in the car and the dashboard appears over the lock screen by itself, once per drive.

How fast it notices depends on power. In a socket that switches off with the key, it wakes as you turn it. Otherwise allow a minute or two.

Switches only you can set

Some phones add switches of their own that no app can ask for or check. If yours has them, set them for this app in your phone's Settings, under Apps. Autostart lets it start watching again after a restart or an update. Show on lock screen and Open new windows while running in the background let it wake the screen.

Settings: where readings come from, what this car reports, and the theme.

Staying up to date

Installed from Google Play, the app keeps itself up to date. An update that lands mid-drive does not lose the trip: the app starts itself again, with Autostart on, and carries on recording.

Leaving the adapter plugged in

The socket has power even with the car off, so an adapter left in keeps drawing a little. Driving daily or every other day, that is fine. Leaving the car a week or more, unplug it — especially with an older battery.

Better adapters can be told to sleep, and the app asks yours to a couple of minutes after you switch off. Cheap ones ignore it. Settings tells you which kind yours is, under Sleep command.

Your data stays yours

No account, no server, no analytics. Nothing is sent anywhere unless you switch on Drive backup, and that goes to your Drive and nobody else's. Your car's VIN is never read and no location is recorded. The details are in the privacy policy.
